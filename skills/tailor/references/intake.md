# Intake — getting the story out of someone's head

People rarely have design vocabulary, but everyone can tell a story about their own thing. The goal
of the intake is to make telling that story easy and a little fun, not to interview them.

## The intake message

Send one short, warm message in the person's language. It contains:

1. One sentence on what you need: "Tell me about the site the way you'd tell a friend — what it's for,
   who it's for, how it should feel. Messy is fine; images in your head count."
2. Two or three example stories (pick ones that are *unlike* their project, so they don't copy the
   shape; vary length and tone). Use the bank below or write new ones.
3. Two optional extras, clearly marked as optional:
   - **Something you like** — a poster, banner, album cover, packaging, a photo of a place, a
     screenshot of any site. "It only has to share a feeling, not be a website."
   - **Something you don't want** — "a site you hate, or just words: 'no corporate blue', 'nothing
     that looks like an app'."

Keep it under ~150 words plus the examples. No numbered questionnaire, no sliders, no list of styles
to pick from — a menu of styles is exactly what makes everyone end up in the same three boxes.

## Example story bank

Pick 2–3. Each shows a different way to tell it (facts, mood, comparison, dislikes).

- "My father has run a hardware shop since 1985. I want a site that feels like walking in: wooden
  shelves, hand-written price labels, the smell of oil. Trustworthy, a bit old-fashioned, but not
  dusty. Mostly older customers checking opening hours and calling us. Please nothing glossy like
  those Instagram shops."
- "I'm a wedding photographer but I hate wedding-y things — no script fonts, no blush pink. My photos
  are grainy, flash at night, people laughing too hard. The site should feel like a late-night
  party, a little chaotic, but the photos are the star."
- "A tiny research lab that studies sleep. Visitors are other scientists and journalists. It should
  feel calm and precise, like a well-kept notebook at 3 a.m. — dark, quiet, very readable. We have
  papers, a team page and a 'join a study' form."
- "Our band plays surf rock in Turkish. Think 1960s Istanbul cinema posters mixed with beach
  holidays. Loud, colourful, hand-made. We need tour dates, songs and a merch link."
- "A one-person accounting firm for freelancers. People are scared of taxes; I want them to feel
  'oh, this person is normal and kind'. Clear, warm, a bit funny, zero stock photos of handshakes."

## After the story: gap questions

Read the story and list what you still need to build an honest page. Ask at most three things, in
one message, and only if the story didn't answer them. Typical gaps:

- **The main action** — what should a visitor do? (call, book, buy, read, sign up, visit)
- **Real content** — what actually exists: texts, menu, prices, project list, photos, logo?
- **Ambiguous mood** — only when the story points two ways ("calm" but also "loud"?). Ask with the
  person's own words: "You said both 'calm' and 'party' — which wins on the first screen?"
- **Must-keep brand assets** — an existing logo or colour they cannot change.

Do not ask about fonts, colours or layout styles directly; those are your job, derived from the
story. If they volunteer them, treat them as strong signals.

## Reading the story

While reading, pull out and note (these feed STYLE-DNA.md):

- **Facts**: what it is, since when, where, who runs it, who visits, what they need from the page.
- **Sensory words**: materials, places, light, sounds, smells, eras ("wooden shelves", "3 a.m.").
- **Comparisons**: films, places, objects, music — these are the richest design material.
- **Tensions**: pairs that make the brand interesting ("old-fashioned but not dusty"). The best
  sites resolve a tension instead of picking the safe middle.
- **Dislikes**: explicit and implied ("nothing glossy" → no gradients, no glassmorphism, no glow).
- **Voice**: how they talk is how the site's copy should talk.
